What each check means

Mail servers that receive email from your domain look up three records to decide whether it's really from you. Together they decide whether fake email gets through.

What happens to fake email (DMARC)
Tells mail servers what to do with email that claims to be from you but fails the other two checks: deliver it anyway ("p=none", watching only), send it to spam ("quarantine"), or refuse it ("reject"). Without it, fake email gets delivered. Start at "p=none" with reports turned on, check that all your real email passes for a few weeks, then move to "quarantine". Only one DMARC record is allowed; with two, mail servers ignore both. The record also says who gets the reports ("rua="): daily emails from mail servers listing every server that sent email as you, and whether it passed. The page shows who that is. If you set up your domain at a company such as GoDaddy, the reports may go to them rather than to you; add your own address so you can see them too.
Who can send email as you (SPF)
A list of the servers allowed to send email for your domain, such as your email service and a newsletter service. Only one SPF record is allowed: with two, mail servers ignore both. The ending says what to do with everyone else: "~all" marks them suspicious, "-all" refuses them, and "+all" lets anyone in, which defeats the point. Each service on the list costs lookups, and past ten the whole record counts as broken. When you stop using a service, take it off the list: GoDaddy's "include:secureserver.net" is the common leftover after email moves elsewhere.
The signature on your email (DKIM)
Your email service signs every message, and a key in your domain's records lets receivers check the signature. You turn it on in your email service, which then gives you the record to add. Each service stores the key under a name of its own choosing, so this page looks where the big services put it. If yours uses another name, the page can't see it, and that may be fine.
Your domain's registration
When it runs out, your website and email stop, and someone else can buy the name. Keep automatic renewal on at the company you bought it from, with a card that's current.

Fixing a record

These records live at your DNS company, the company that runs your domain's settings (often Cloudflare, or GoDaddy if you bought the domain there). Your email service gives you the exact values to add. Have a saved copy of your records? Almanac Check compares every one with what's live.

What this page sends, and to whom

To see your records, the page asks the internet, just as a mail server does before it accepts email from you. Here is everything it asks, and who gets each question.

What goes out

  • Your domain's name, with the kinds of record it asks for: who receives your email, your text records (where SPF lives), and your DMARC record.
  • A few names where email services keep their signature key, such as google._domainkey.yourbusiness.com.
  • The names of the services your SPF record lists, such as _spf.google.com, to count the lookups they cost.

Who gets it

  • Google's public lookup service (dns.google) gets the names above. The page asks Google not to pass on what area you're in.
  • IANA (data.iana.org), the body that keeps the list of domain registries, sends that list. It isn't told your domain.
  • Your domain's registry, the company that keeps the master list for your domain ending (for .com, that's Verisign), gets your domain's name, to say when the registration runs out.

Like any lookup, each of them sees the names asked about and your internet address. The questions travel over a secure connection, so others on your network can see only that you reached Google and the registry. None of this is secret to begin with: these records are public by design, so mail servers everywhere can read them.

What it doesn't do

  • It doesn't send or read any email, and doesn't touch your mailbox or your settings.
  • Nothing you check is sent to FreeOT. Opening this page loads it from FreeOT's site, like any web page, but the page has no tracking scripts or cookies and stores nothing.
  • A shared result link carries only your domain, after the "#" in the address, which browsers never send to the server.

Is this page the same as the code on GitHub?

It's meant to be, file for file: there's no build step, so the site serves the files in the public repository as they are, and every change is made there, where its history is public. Two ways to check:

  1. Right-click this page, choose View page source, and compare it with public/tools/email-check/index.html on GitHub. That one comparison covers everything the page runs: the source lists a fingerprint of every script and style it uses, and your browser refuses to run any file that doesn't match its fingerprint. Up-to-date browsers check them; an older one skips the check.
  2. Watch what it sends. In Chrome or Edge, press F12 (on a Mac, Command+Option+I), open the Network tab, then check a domain. Besides this page's own files, you'll see requests only to dns.google, data.iana.org and your registry.
