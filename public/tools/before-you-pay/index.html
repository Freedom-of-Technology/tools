Before you pay

Four scams to check for before you pay

The FTC and the FBI warn small businesses about these four. Each one arrives looking like an ordinary bill or email, and each is stopped by one habit. Read them here, or print this page for the wall where bills get paid.

Nothing to type, nothing kept. This page asks you for nothing and sends nothing anywhere. What this page sends, and to whom.

The four scams

A fake invoice

A bill for something you never ordered, such as a directory listing, a "renewal" of your web address, or office supplies. Sometimes a call comes first, to "confirm" an order.

Pay only bills you can match to something you ordered. If you're unsure, check with the company using contact details you already have, never the ones on the bill.

FTC: Scams and your small business

Changed bank details

An email from a supplier, or from an address that looks like theirs, saying their bank has changed and asking you to pay the next bill into a new account.

Before changing anyone's payment details, call them on a number you already have, never one in the message.

FBI: Business email compromise

A gift-card request

An urgent message that seems to come from the owner or a manager, asking someone to buy gift cards and send the codes.

Only scammers ask to be paid with gift cards. Check with the person face to face, or on a number you already have.

FTC: No, that's not your boss asking

A fake sign-in page

A message says your account is locked, a payment failed, or your password needs updating. Its link opens a page that looks like the real one and asks you to sign in or pay.

Don't sign in or pay from a link in a message. Go to the site yourself, or call the company on a number you know is real.

FTC: How to recognize phishing

If one already got through

  • You paid: call your bank right away and ask them to recall the payment. Hours matter. Then report it to the FBI at ic3.gov, whatever the amount.
  • You bought gift cards: contact the company that issued them right away, and keep the cards and the receipt.
  • You signed in: change the password, sign out of every device, turn on two-step sign-in, and check that the recovery email and phone number are yours.
  • Every time: report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

More on these scams

Why a habit, rather than signs to spot
A good fake looks exactly like the real thing: the right logo, a name you know, an address one letter off. Checking through contact details you already have works however good the fake is, because the scammer can't answer that phone.
Things you didn't order
The FTC says a business can keep things it was sent but never ordered, without paying for them. Don't agree to anything on a call that asks you to "confirm" an order or a listing. FTC: Scams and your small business
Gift cards
Scammers ask for gift cards because the money is quick to take and hard to get back. Once someone has the card's number and PIN, the money is theirs. FTC: Gift card scams
Getting a payment back
A bank can't always recall a payment, and the sooner it's asked, the better its chances. Report a payment that went out to the FBI at ic3.gov as well as to your bank, whatever the amount. FBI: Business email compromise
After a fake sign-in
Whoever has your password can change it, and the recovery email and phone, so you can't get back in. Change it first, then check what else they could reach, starting with your email. FTC: Recovering a hacked account
Teaching your team
The FTC's printed guide, Scams and Your Small Business, covers these and more, and the FTC sends copies free. CISA, the government's cybersecurity agency, has advice on teaching staff to spot fake emails. FTC: the guide (PDF), free printed copies, CISA: Teach employees to avoid phishing

What this page sends, and to whom

Nothing. There's nothing to type on this page, and once it has loaded it doesn't contact FreeOT or anyone else.

What it doesn't do

  • It has no tracking scripts or cookies, and stores nothing. Opening the page loads it from FreeOT's site, like any web page.
  • Printing happens in your browser's own print window. Nothing about it reaches FreeOT.
  • The links to the FTC, the FBI and CISA open their own sites in a new tab. What those sites see is up to them.
  • It can't connect anywhere even if it tried: its security settings tell your browser to block every connection the page itself might make.

Is this page the same as the code on GitHub?

It's meant to be, file for file: there's no build step, so the site serves the files in the public repository as they are, and every change is made there, where its history is public. To check, right-click this page, choose View page source, and compare it with public/tools/before-you-pay/index.html on GitHub. The source lists a fingerprint of every script and style it uses, and your browser refuses to run any file that doesn't match its fingerprint. Up-to-date browsers check them; an older one skips the check.
