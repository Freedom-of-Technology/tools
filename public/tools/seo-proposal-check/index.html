Red flags in an SEO agency's proposal

An SEO agency promises to help your business come up when people search. Most proposals sound alike, and the ones that cost you tend to hide it in the same few places: a promise no one can keep, your accounts in the agency's name, a contract that's hard to leave. SEO proposals, in plain words

What SEO is
SEO, short for search engine optimization, is work that helps your business come up when people search: on your website, your Google profile, your listings and your reviews. Much of it is steady housekeeping, and a good agency can save you time. Google's own guide to hiring an SEO is short, and worth reading before you meet one.
What a red flag means
Something to ask about before you sign, not proof of a bad agency. Some have a fair answer: a few months up front, for instance, can be reasonable. Ask for the answers in writing, so you have a record of what you were told, and ask for anything that matters to be in the contract itself.
What a sound proposal says
  • Every account stays in your business's name: your Google profile, website, web address, ads, Search Console and Google Analytics. The agency is added as a user, and taken off when you part ways.
  • What they'll do each month, in a list you can check.
  • Reports from your own Search Console, and the calls or bookings that came in, not a score of their own.
  • What results they expect, and by when, with no promise of a ranking.
  • How to leave, and what you keep when you do.
Your accounts
Much of what goes wrong when a business leaves an agency comes down to whose name the accounts are in. The Ownership Checklist goes through every account your business runs on, and Who owns your Google profile? checks your Google profile, an account agencies often hold.
Your contract
This page can't tell you what a contract or a law means for your business. If something worries you, ask a lawyer before you sign.
The questions
Nothing on this page is saved, not even your ticks. Copy the questions to send them to the agency, or to keep beside the proposal.

What this page sends, and to whom

Nothing. Once this page has loaded, your ticks and the questions stay in your browser, and the page doesn't contact FreeOT, any agency or anyone else.

What it doesn't do

  • It never asks for the proposal, the agency's name or your business's name. You tick boxes; nothing is uploaded.
  • It doesn't put your ticks in this page's address, so nothing can be shared or saved by accident.
  • The links to Google's and the FTC's pages open in a new tab, and take nothing from this page with them.
  • It has no tracking scripts or cookies, and stores nothing. Opening the page loads it from FreeOT's site, like any web page.
  • It can't connect anywhere even if it tried: its security settings tell your browser to block every connection the page itself might make.

Is this page the same as the code on GitHub?

It's meant to be, file for file: there's no build step, so the site serves the files in the public repository as they are, and every change is made there, where its history is public. Two ways to check:

  1. Right-click this page, choose View page source, and compare it with public/tools/seo-proposal-check/index.html on GitHub. That one comparison covers everything the page runs: the source lists a fingerprint of every script and style it uses, and your browser refuses to run any file that doesn't match its fingerprint. Up-to-date browsers check them; an older one skips the check.
  2. Watch what it sends. In Chrome or Edge, press F12 (on a Mac, Command+Option+I), open the Network tab, then tick a few boxes. You'll see nothing go out.
