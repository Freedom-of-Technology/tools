The Ownership Checklist

Is your business's technology in your name?

Ten accounts every small business should hold in its own name, from your web address to your passwords. Say who holds each one now, and this page writes your plan: who to ask for what, with the message to send, and what to find out first. It takes a few minutes, in three short steps, and it asks only what you already know.

Who holds each one?

In my name means the account belongs to you or your business, and you can sign in to it yourself. An account in an employee's, a relative's or your web designer's name is someone else's, even if they'd hand it over.

Using your result

Why it matters
Most small businesses run on accounts someone else set up: a web designer, a former employee, a relative who was good with computers. Nothing goes wrong until that person moves on, and then a renewal lapses, a password can't be reset, or a listing can't be changed. Getting each account into your name while everyone is still friendly is far easier than getting it back later.
Where to start
Your result starts with your web address and your email. Whoever holds those two can reset the passwords for almost everything else, so they come first. Then it lists who to ask for what, what to find out first, and what to set up, each in the checklist's order.
Asking for an account
Ask in writing, and kindly: most people who set things up meant well. Your result writes the message for you, one for each person, written for who they are to you and asking for every account they hold. Show the message to read it before you copy it. It asks for each account to be moved into your name, or for an owner login of your own, rather than for their password.
Once it's yours: change the password, which signs out other devices on most services. Check that the recovery email and phone number are yours, not the person who handed it over. Put your own payment card on it, and check when it renews. Turn on two-step sign-in, and keep the recovery codes somewhere safe, such as a password manager.
Agreements and open bills
If you have an agreement with whoever holds an account, such as a web designer, an agency or an IT company, it may say what's yours and when it's handed over to you. Check it before you ask, especially if you're still working together or have a bill open with them. This page can't tell you what your agreement means.
Sharing your result
Your result comes with a link. It holds your answers, so whoever you send it to, such as the person who helps with your technology, sees the same plan. The answers ride after the # in the link, the part your browser never sends to any website.
This checklist and an audit
This checklist asks what you know. An ownership audit finds out: it starts from the public records for your web address and every account your business runs on, including ones you've forgotten, then gets each one into your name and writes it all down.

What can go wrong, account by account

Moving an account into your name is usually simple. A few mistakes can't be undone: they come first, marked, and your plan shows them too. Each links to the company's own help page.

What this page sends, and to whom

Nothing. Once this page has loaded, your answers and your result stay in your browser, and the page doesn't contact FreeOT or anyone else.

What it doesn't do

  • It doesn't look anything up. It never asks for your web address, your business's name, anyone's name or any login. "Who holds it?" is a choice such as "A web designer or agency", never a name.
  • It doesn't send your answers anywhere. When you see your result, your answers are added after the # in the page's address, so you can share it; browsers never send that part to the website.
  • It has no tracking scripts or cookies, and stores nothing. Opening the page loads it from FreeOT's site, like any web page.
  • It can't connect anywhere even if it tried: its security settings tell your browser to block every connection the page itself might make.

Is this page the same as the code on GitHub?

It's meant to be, file for file: there's no build step, so the site serves the files in the public repository as they are, and every change is made there, where its history is public. Two ways to check:

  1. Right-click this page, choose View page source, and compare it with public/tools/ownership-checklist/index.html on GitHub. That one comparison covers everything the page runs: the source lists a fingerprint of every script and style it uses, and your browser refuses to run any file that doesn't match its fingerprint. Up-to-date browsers check them; an older one skips the check.
  2. Watch what it sends. In Chrome or Edge, press F12 (on a Mac, Command+Option+I), open the Network tab, then see your result. You'll see nothing go out.
