Using your code

Try it first
Point your phone's camera at the code on your screen. Your phone should offer to add a contact, with your details filled in. Every code this page offers has already passed the same test: the page scans it back and checks it holds exactly what you typed.
Which file to use
  • Card picture: save it to your phone's photos and open it when someone asks for your details. It shows your logo, your name and the code.
  • Code, SVG: for anything printed, such as business cards, flyers and signs. It stays sharp at any size; send this one to your printer or designer.
  • Code, PNG: for documents, slides and email.
  • Contact file (.vcf): the same contact as a file. Attach it to an email, or open it to add yourself to your own contacts.
Printing it
Keep it at least 2 cm (about 3/4 inch) wide, and keep the white border around it: phones need that border to find the code. Black on white scans best. Test a printed copy with your phone before ordering a big batch.
Why it never expires
Many QR code websites make "dynamic" codes: the code holds a link to their server, which then sends people on. When the subscription ends, the code stops working, often after it's printed on everything. This code holds your contact itself, so there's nothing to stop. The one trade-off: if your details change, make a new code.
Your logo
Any size works. A large logo, such as one from your designer or a photo, is more than a card needs, so your browser makes a smaller copy of it and every file you download uses that copy. That keeps the files small enough to email or send to a printer. Your own file isn't changed.
A logo in the middle
QR codes carry spare information so they still read when part is scuffed or covered. With your logo in the middle, the page uses the most spare information a code can carry, keeps the logo small, and offers the code only if it still scans. Because it's small there, a fifth of the code's width, small words and thin lines in your logo won't show; your card picture shows it larger. A busy or very dark logo can still make a code harder to read on older phones, so try it before printing.

What this page sends, and to whom

Nothing. Once this page has loaded, it makes your code, your card picture and your files in your browser, and doesn't contact FreeOT or anyone else.

What it doesn't do

  • It doesn't upload your details or your logo. Your browser reads the logo from your computer or phone to draw it; the downloads are made in your browser, too.
  • It has no tracking scripts or cookies, and stores nothing. Opening the page loads it from FreeOT's site, like any web page.
  • It can't connect anywhere even if it tried: its security settings tell your browser to block every connection the page itself might make.

Is this page the same as the code on GitHub?

It's meant to be, file for file: there's no build step, so the site serves the files in the public repository as they are, and every change is made there, where its history is public. Two ways to check:

  1. Right-click this page, choose View page source, and compare it with public/tools/contact-card/index.html on GitHub. That one comparison covers everything the page runs: the source lists a fingerprint of every script and style it uses, and your browser refuses to run any file that doesn't match its fingerprint. Up-to-date browsers check them; an older one skips the check.
  2. Watch what it sends. In Chrome or Edge, press F12 (on a Mac, Command+Option+I), open the Network tab, then make a code. You'll see nothing go out: what's listed is your own browser making the downloads, at addresses starting blob: or data:.

The QR code is made with qrcode-generator and test-scanned with jsQR, two widely used free libraries, copied into the repository as published.
