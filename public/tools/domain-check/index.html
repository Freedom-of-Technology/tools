About records files

What a records file is

Your domain's DNS records are a short list of settings kept by your DNS company: where your website is, which service gets your email, and the codes that let other services prove they work for you. A records file is that list, saved as plain text. Save one while everything works, and you can always see what changed, and put it back.

Saving one from Cloudflare

  1. Sign in to Cloudflare and click your domain.
  2. Open DNS, then Records.
  3. Click Import and Export, then Export.
  4. The file lands in your Downloads folder. Keep it somewhere safe.

Saving one from GoDaddy

  1. Sign in to GoDaddy and open your Domain Portfolio.
  2. Click your domain, then open DNS.
  3. Open the Actions menu and choose Export Zone File.
  4. The file lands in your Downloads folder. Keep it somewhere safe.

Another DNS company? Look in its DNS settings for "export" or "zone file". Most offer one.

What each result means

Matches
What's live is the same as your file. Nothing to do.
Different
What's live isn't what your file says. If you or someone helping you changed it on purpose, save a new records file. If nobody did, change the record at your DNS company back to the value in your file. Just changed it? Lookups can show the old answer for a while; the result says how long.
Missing
Nothing is live for this record. If nobody removed it on purpose, add it back at your DNS company with the value in your file. A brand-new record can take up to an hour to show up.
Cloudflare answers
The record has Cloudflare's orange cloud on, so Cloudflare answers with its own addresses and where it really points can't be seen from outside. It's there and working.
GoDaddy website
GoDaddy's website builder answers for it. It's there and working.
Copy line differs
Only in files kept by FreeOT. A note in the file that writes a long value out in one piece, for copying, doesn't say the same as the record under it. One of them is wrong.
Which DNS company runs your domain
The registry, where your domain is listed, says a different DNS company runs it than your file does. If you moved it on purpose, save a new records file. If you didn't, someone may have changed your domain: get help right away.
DNSSEC
A lock on your records that some DNS companies turn on. The registry and your file disagree about whether it's on. If nobody changed it on purpose, get help.
Not checked
A kind of record this page can't look up, or names that are live but aren't in your file. DNS doesn't list every name a domain has; your DNS company's export does.
Couldn't check
The lookup didn't work this time. Try again in a minute.

What this page sends, and to whom

Your file stays on your computer. To see what's live, the page has to ask the internet, just as your browser does every time it opens a website. Here is everything it asks, and who gets each question.

What goes out

  • For each record in your file, two things: its name (such as www.yourbusiness.com) and its type (such as the one for email). What your file says the record should be, and any notes in it, are never sent. The page compares the answer with your file right here, on your computer.
  • Your domain's name, once, to find out which DNS company runs it.
  • Nothing at all from a file that looks like it holds a secret, such as a password, a private key or an access code. The page checks for those first, and refuses the file before asking anything. Secrets belong in a password manager, not a records file.

Who gets it

  • Google's public lookup service (dns.google) gets the names and types. The page asks Google not to pass on what area you're in.
  • IANA (data.iana.org), the body that keeps the list of domain registries, sends that list. It isn't told your domain.
  • Your domain's registry, the company that keeps the master list for your domain ending (for .com, that's Verisign), gets your domain's name.

Like any lookup, each of them sees the names asked about and your internet address. The questions travel over a secure connection, so others on your network can see only that you reached Google and the registry, not which names you asked about.

None of this is secret to begin with. DNS records are public by design: anyone can look up any domain's records at any time. That's how email and websites find you.

What it doesn't do

  • It doesn't check whether domains are for sale, and doesn't search for new ones. It only looks up the records of a domain that's already yours.
  • Nothing you check is sent to FreeOT. Opening this page loads it from FreeOT's site, like any web page, but the page has no tracking scripts or cookies and stores nothing.

Is this page the same as the code on GitHub?

It's meant to be, file for file: there's no build step, so the site serves the files in the public repository as they are, and every change is made there, where its history is public. You don't have to take our word for it. Two ways to check:

  1. Right-click this page, choose View page source, and compare it with public/tools/domain-check/index.html on GitHub. That one comparison covers everything the page runs: the source lists a fingerprint of every script and style it uses, and your browser refuses to run any file that doesn't match its fingerprint. Up-to-date browsers check them; an older one skips the check.
  2. Watch what it sends. In Chrome or Edge, press F12 (on a Mac, Command+Option+I), open the Network tab, then check a file. Besides this page's own files, you'll see requests only to dns.google, data.iana.org and your registry.
