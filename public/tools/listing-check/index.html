Your listings, in plain words

Why it matters
A customer who finds an old phone number or address may give up rather than look further. Google also says how well known a business is counts toward where it shows in local results, judged partly from what it finds about the business across the web, directories included. No one outside Google knows how much any one listing counts, so treat this as housekeeping, not an emergency. Google: how local results are ranked
What counts as right
A customer reading it would reach you: your current name, a phone you answer, your address or the area you serve, and your hours. If a listing shows a business that has closed or moved, mark it Wrong.
Your website
Check the details a customer reads, usually in the footer and on the contact page. Many sites also tell search engines their details in a block of data visitors don't see. See what it tells search engines opens schema.org's free checker with your web address: if it lists a LocalBusiness, or a kind of one such as Dentist, its name, address and phone should match yours. If it lists none, your site doesn't say, and whoever looks after it can add it. Google asks for at least the name and address, and suggests the phone, hours and web address. Google: local business details on your site. Google's own Rich Results Test gives a second opinion.
Fixing a listing
Most sites let a business owner claim its listing, then check it's really you, often with a phone call, a text, a postcard or a short video. Claim each one with an account the business owns, such as a shared business email, rather than someone's personal one, so it stays yours when people move on. The Ownership Checklist covers why, and Who owns your Google profile? what to do if someone else holds your Google listing.
When a look-up finds nothing
Facebook, Nextdoor and Angi have no search you can open without signing in, so their look-up is a Google search of that site. Google may not show every page, so if it finds nothing, search the site itself before marking it Not there. Data Axle and Foursquare open their own search page, where you type your phone number or name.
The data suppliers
Data Axle and Foursquare supply business details to other apps and websites, so a mistake there can turn up in places you'd never think to check. A third that's often named, Localeze, is now a paid TransUnion service sold mainly through resellers, so it isn't on the list.
Your summary
Nothing on this page is saved, not even your marks. Copy the summary to keep your list, or to send it to whoever helps with your listings; it lists what to fix and where.

What this page sends, and to whom

Nothing, by itself. What you type stays in your browser, and the page doesn't contact FreeOT or anyone else. Only the links you choose to open go anywhere, each in a new tab:

  • Look it up opens that site's own search, with your business name and town in its address, as if you'd typed them there yourself. That site sees the search, as it would any search. For Facebook, Nextdoor and Angi it's a Google search, so Google sees it.
  • See what it tells search engines opens schema.org's checker with your web address, and it reads that page of your site. Open your website opens your own site.
  • Fix it opens the site's page for business owners. Nothing you typed goes with it.

What it doesn't do

  • It never sends your phone number or street address anywhere. They're only on this page, and in the summary if you copy it.
  • It doesn't put your details in this page's address, so nothing can be shared or saved by accident.
  • The links don't tell those sites you came from FreeOT: the page asks your browser not to say where you came from.
  • It has no tracking scripts or cookies, and stores nothing. Opening the page loads it from FreeOT's site, like any web page.
  • It can't connect anywhere even if it tried: its security settings tell your browser to block every connection the page itself might make.

Is this page the same as the code on GitHub?

It's meant to be, file for file: there's no build step, so the site serves the files in the public repository as they are, and every change is made there, where its history is public. Two ways to check:

  1. Right-click this page, choose View page source, and compare it with public/tools/listing-check/index.html on GitHub. That one comparison covers everything the page runs: the source lists a fingerprint of every script and style it uses, and your browser refuses to run any file that doesn't match its fingerprint. Up-to-date browsers check them; an older one skips the check.
  2. Watch what it sends. In Chrome or Edge, press F12 (on a Mac, Command+Option+I), open the Network tab, then make your list and mark it. You'll see nothing go out.
